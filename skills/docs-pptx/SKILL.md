# Skill: docs-pptx

## Purpose
Produce a slide deck the way a paper is produced, by **compiling a plain-text source into the
artifact** rather than dragging boxes until it looks right. A deck built this way can be linted
before it is shown, timed against the slot it has to fill, cited from a single bibliography, and
rebuilt from what is checked in. This skill owns the deck as a build product. It does not own what a
figure may contain (`docs-figure`) or the prose inside it (`writing-style`).

## When to Use
- Building slides for a talk, seminar, reading group or defence, especially one with a fixed slot.
- Generating slides from code, from a results table, or from research notes.
- A deck has to stay editable by a co-author, be sent to a venue, or pass accessibility tooling.
- Text ran off the bottom of a slide, or a talk written for thirty minutes ran forty.
- Deciding how a talk cites its sources, and where the figures come from.

**Not for**: what a figure may contain or which plotting pipeline to use, which is `docs-figure`.
Not for the prose rules the slides and notes are written in, which is `writing-style`. Not for the
research that fills the deck, and not for posters or papers.

## Why compile a deck at all

Marp, reveal.js and pandoc all turn text into slides, and all of them treat PowerPoint as an export
format rather than a target. What comes out is a stack of pictures. That is fine until a co-author
wants to fix a typo, a venue asks for the file, or somebody runs it through accessibility tooling.
Emit text boxes containing text and tables that are tables, and pictures only where there is
genuinely a picture.

The deeper reason is that **typesetting fails silently**. A wrong colour is visible the moment you
look. Text three lines past the bottom of its box is visible only from the back row of the room,
because the tool that made it was happy and the person who made it was reading the source. A
compiled deck can be asked how much of each box the content used, and that question is the whole
justification for the pipeline.

## What belongs in the source

One file, readable as a document before it is a deck. Markdown with three additions and no more:
slides separated by a rule at column zero, per-slide options in a comment no markdown renderer will
show, and fenced containers for the structure markdown has no syntax for.

Resist inventing a slide language. Every construct you add is one a co-author has to learn, and the
ones that earn their place are few. The container that repeatedly does earn it is a **labelled panel
block**, because the recurring shape in a research talk is "here is the thing, here is what counts
as correct, here is what actually happened", and three unlabelled paragraphs do not carry that.

Two things belong in the source that authors routinely leave out. The **declared length of the slot**,
because without it no timing check is possible. And the **speaker notes**, because they are the only
honest input to a timing estimate and because a deck whose notes are missing cannot be handed to
anyone else.

## Citations: one bibliography, cited by key

Slides cite by key against one bibliography file. Nothing about a citation is typed twice, so
correcting an author list or a venue fixes every slide that rests on it, and the references slide is
generated from the keys the deck actually cited rather than maintained by hand and quietly going
stale.

Fix the rendered form once and apply it everywhere. A short form that names the work, its first two
authors, the main affiliation and the venue is enough for a footer. For fast-moving preprints record
the **year and month**, because in a field where the relevant work is four months old a bare year
carries no information.

An unknown cite key is a build error, not a warning. A talk that cites a paper it cannot name is
worse than one that does not cite it, and the failure should surface at build time rather than on
stage. Validate the keys **before** the expensive asset pass, so a one-character typo does not cost
a minute of figure downloads before it is reported.

Two shapes defeat naive surname extraction and both appear in real bibliographies. Corporate authors
have no surname, so "Asta Team" becomes a paper credited to somebody called Team, and the fix is an
explicit display override rather than a cleverer parser. Stacked nobiliary particles need the
surname to run from the first particle to the end, so a single fixed lookback turns "van den Oord"
into "den Oord".

## Figures: clip the paper's own

Where a paper has already drawn the thing, show that drawing. Redrawing someone's pipeline diagram
introduces errors and throws away the authority of showing the original, and the audience knows the
difference. So the pipeline needs a crop primitive: **four fractions of the source image**, left, top,
right and bottom, producing a clipped panel as a build artifact cached by a hash of the file and the
crop.

The crop is what makes this work rather than merely possible. A published figure is usually two or
four panels and a slide is 16:9, so pasting the whole figure wastes most of the slide and shrinks the
part that matters to illegibility. Crop to the panel the slide actually argues about, land the crop
on a panel boundary rather than through a label, and aim for something close to 2:1 or wider.

Diagrams with no published original are written as text in the source and rendered by a diagram tool
into the same cache. Push the deck's palette down into that renderer. A diagram in the tool's stock
colours announces itself as a different document from the slide it sits on.

## The preview that lies

This is the part that is re-derived wrongly every time, so it is worth stating plainly.

**Layout must run once.** Produce a list of placed rectangles, fully resolved, with every colour a
hex string, every size a point value and every rectangle in absolute units. Then let each backend
paint that same list. A second renderer with its own opinions about line breaking will eventually
disagree with the first, and you will not find out which one was right until the room does.

**Line breaks are decided by font metrics, so a preview in the wrong font is not a preview.** On a
box with no fonts installed, a browser substitutes whatever it can find, and the substitute in this
family's case was 25 percent wider than the font the deck asked for. Every block was measured for one
font and painted in another, and text blocks overlapped. The fix is not a fudge factor, it is to
install a **metric-compatible** substitute and make the deck ask for the font that substitute matches.

**Measure the metrics, do not reason about them.** A hand-written width table that grouped characters
into plausible classes was five percent short on whole sentences, because it lumped `r`, `t` and `f`
in with `i` as narrow. Five percent is enough to predict one line where the renderer produces two.
Render the printable character set at a large size in the target font, divide by the em, and use the
measured table. Do the same for the bold face separately, because the bold ratio is not uniform.

Three specific errors, each of which cost a visible defect here:

| error | what it looks like | why |
|---|---|---|
| paragraph weight not reaching the width calculation | a bold title sits on top of its own underline | the title is one plain run inside a bold paragraph, so per-run weight alone measures it 4% narrow |
| hanging indent not subtracted from the measuring width | a callout lands on the last line of the bullets above it | both backends indent the whole paragraph body by indent plus hang, so a bullet measured at `width - indent` is one glyph box too wide |
| line height treated as a multiple of the em | every box is a fifth too short and blocks collide | a normal single-spaced line is about 1.2 em, and the presentation format's percentage multiplies that, not the em |

Keep a small explicit safety margin on top of the measured table, and say in a comment that it is a
margin. An estimate that breaks a line slightly early costs a little whitespace. An estimate that
breaks slightly late costs an overlap.

**The output format's XML has a fixed child order and the reader rejects the file outright when it is
wrong.** Paragraph properties and table cell properties both have one. Where a library gives you no
API and you are writing elements by hand, assert the order in a test rather than trusting that it
looked fine when you opened it.

## The gates a deck build must have

A build that writes a wrong deck and exits zero defeats the entire pipeline, so **an error-severity
finding must fail the build**. Offer a flag to override it, never a default that hides it.

| gate | catches |
|---|---|
| overflow | content past the bottom of its box, after any auto-shrink |
| shrunk | auto-shrink dropped the body far enough that the slide should be split |
| long title | a title that wraps and eats a line of body, with the threshold measured rather than guessed |
| missing image | a figure path that does not resolve, named rather than silently blank |
| uncited number | a slide stating a percentage, a multiple or a large count with no cite key |
| timing | total estimated minutes against the declared slot |

The uncited-number gate is the one that keeps a talk honest under questioning, and it is cheap.
The timing gate is the one that changes the deck: it reads the speaker notes at a deliberately slow
words-per-minute, so a slot that plans for a fast reading becomes a talk that overruns.

## Reviewing a deck you cannot open

A generated deck must be **looked at**, and the machine that generated it usually has no PowerPoint.
Rasterise every slide through a headless browser from the same placed geometry, and assemble a
contact sheet. One image showing every slide at once is how a broken deck is spotted in a single
glance, and it is the only review that catches the failures a linter has no rule for: a figure that
is technically inside its box but too small to read, a slide that is correct and dead, a section that
has three slides where it needed one.

Do this at least once per major edit, not once at the end.

## Rules

1. **The deliverable is editable.** Text boxes contain text, tables are tables, pictures appear only
   where there is a picture. A deck exported as images cannot be corrected by the person holding it.
2. **Layout runs once and both backends paint the same placed geometry.** Two renderers drift, and
   the drift is discovered in the room.
3. **The preview renders in a metric-compatible font, or it is not a preview.** Ask for a font whose
   metrics you measured and whose substitute you installed.
4. **Character widths are measured, not classified by hand.** Measure the printable set in the target
   font, and measure the bold face separately.
5. **Paragraph-level weight and hanging indent both enter the width calculation.** Measuring runs
   alone, or subtracting the indent alone, under-counts lines and produces overlaps.
6. **An error-severity lint finding fails the build.** An override flag is fine, an override default
   is not.
7. **Slides cite by key from one bibliography, and an unknown key is a build error.** Validate keys
   before the asset pass so a typo does not cost a download round.
8. **Preprints are cited with year and month.** In a fast field a bare year does not identify the work.
9. **Show the paper's own figure, cropped to the panel the slide argues about.** Crops are fractions
   of the source, cached by content, and land on panel boundaries.
10. **Every slide that carries an argument carries speaker notes**, because notes are the timing input
    and the handover artifact.
11. **The slot length is declared in the source.** Without it there is no timing check, only a hope.
12. **Look at the rendered slides.** Rasterise to a contact sheet at least once per major edit.

## Anti-patterns

- **The second renderer.** A preview written independently of the output backend. It agrees for a
  week, then one of them changes how it wraps and the other becomes a confident lie.
- **The fudge factor.** A magic multiplier added to text measurement until the overlap went away.
  It hides a systematic error that will reappear at a different font size. Measure the metrics and
  correct the bias by name.
- **The whole figure.** A four-panel published figure pasted at full width so that every panel is
  unreadable, when the slide argues about one of them.
- **The redrawn diagram.** Someone's architecture figure rebuilt by hand, subtly wrong and stripped of
  the authority of the original.
- **Notes as bullet echo.** Speaker notes that restate the slide text. They inflate the timing
  estimate, help no one rehearse, and cannot be handed to a colleague.
- **The green exit code.** A build that reports findings and returns success anyway, so the Makefile,
  the CI job and the author all believe the deck is fine.
- **The bibliography retyped onto a slide.** One author list corrected in the source and not in the
  three footers that quote it.
